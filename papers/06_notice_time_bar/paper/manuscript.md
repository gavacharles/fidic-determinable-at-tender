# "Became Aware, or Should Have Become Aware": Objective Awareness Dates and the FIDIC Notice Time Bar

Gavamukulya Charles

*Draft 0.1, 15 October 2026. Paper 6 of "Determinable at tender". Target: International Construction Law Review; Journal of Legal Affairs and Dispute Resolution in Engineering and Construction (ASCE).*

## Abstract

**Introduction.** FIDIC Sub-Clause 20.1 (2017: 20.2.1) bars a claim unless notice is given within 28 days after the Contractor "became aware, or should have become aware" of the event. The time bar is a condition precedent, and the Engineers in the cases studied treat it as such. Its start date, constructive awareness, is not defined. This paper asks whether, for events that are observable in data, an objective awareness date exists, and whether the contract allows a contractor to know it.

**Methods.** Using the claim record of a 97 km Ugandan design-and-build road package (Project A, 2019–2023), three event types are examined.

1. **Exceptionally adverse climate under the Ministry of Works formula (Clause 1229).** Daily CHIRPS rainfall at Masindi with the contractor's 2005–2017 baseline is used to date the first sign of excess, the month end and the publication of the gridded record, and to track the running entitlement.
2. **Late possession.** The due date of each kilometre is computed under three readings of the access schedule and compared with satellite-dated clearance.
3. **The rhythm of the Contractor's possession notices.**

**Results.**

- **Weather.** Positive monthly entitlement arose in 23 of 36 months. A 28-day clock started at the first excess rain day expires before the gridded record is published in 52% of those months. Because the clause entitles the algebraic sum over the whole contract, the entitlement peaked at 48 days (June 2021) and ended at 38.4. Of 66.8 positive days accrued month by month, 28.4 were later cancelled: entitlement is knowable only at the end of the contract.
- **Possession.** The access schedule ("approximately 5 km sections over a period of 546 days") gives due dates differing by a median of 304 days (maximum 600) per kilometre across three defensible readings.
- **Notices.** The Contractor gave rolling "notices to claim" for pending land every 19–49 days (median 24.5), two years after the contractual date for full access.

**Discussion.** For each event the contract makes awareness a matter of construction, not observation. Contractors respond with blanket and rolling notices, and Engineers with lenient application (EOT 3 granted weather days for September 2019–November 2020 on a submission dated January 2021). The time bar thus generates paper rather than early warning. A "notice clock" that defines awareness by reference to named data releases and dates already in the Contract would make the condition precedent determinable:

- the publication of the month's record for weather;
- the scheduled date per section for access;
- the release date for indices.

## 1. Introduction

The notice time bar is the most consequential procedural rule in FIDIC. Under the 1999 Sub-Clause 20.1, if the Contractor fails to give notice "not later than 28 days after the Contractor became aware, or should have become aware, of the event or circumstance", the Time for Completion is not extended and the Employer is discharged. The 2017 edition keeps the 28 days (20.2.1) but softens the consequence: the DAAB or arbitral tribunal may consider late notice (20.2.5).

The Engineer's assessments in the files studied open each claim with the same check: "Issue of Contractor's notice of claim not later than 28 days after the Contractor became aware, or should have become aware". The Employer's response to a DAB referral in the same programme argues that "the Contractor did not meet the time bar".

The bar's start date is *constructive* awareness. Doctrinal writing (e.g., *Obrascon v Gibraltar* [2014] EWHC 1028 (TCC), on the event *or its effect*) treats it case by case. For many events, however, the facts that constitute the event are recorded in data with known publication dates:

- rainfall;
- index publications;
- contractual access dates;
- public-health orders.

For such events, an objective awareness date should be computable. This paper asks whether it is, and what the contract itself does to it.

## 2. Data

- **Project A** (anonymised): design and build of three road sections (97 km), FIDIC Yellow Book 1999 with MoWT/UNRA particular conditions. Commencement 25 April 2019; Time for Completion 1,099 days.
  - **Access schedule** (Appendix to Bid): access to 30% of the remaining Site within 91 days of commencement, and "the remaining parts in approximately 5 km sections over a period of 546 days". Addendum 1 moved the first-section milestone to 16 March 2021.
  - **Claims:** EOT 3 (submitted 4 January 2021; weather September 2019–November 2020, possession, COVID-19), EOT 5 and EOT 7. EOT 7 lists the Contractor's notices to claim (letters 2687–2904, October 2022–February 2023).
- **Rainfall.** CHIRPS v2 daily at Masindi (as in Paper 0), with the Contractor's 2005–2017 baseline.
- **Clearance.** Sentinel-2 clearance month per km for the Masindi–Biiso lot (Paper 2).

## 3. Methods

**Weather (MoWT Clause 1229).**

- Monthly V = (N − Nₙ) + (R − Rₙ)/20, floored at −Nₙ, where N is the count of days ≥ 10 mm and R the monthly rainfall. EOT is the algebraic sum of V over the period for completion.
- For each month, three candidate awareness dates:
  - (a) the first day on which the month's rain-day count exceeds Nₙ;
  - (b) the month end, when V is computable from a site gauge;
  - (c) publication of the month's gridded record (CHIRPS final, about the 20th of the following month).
- The running sum of V tracks when the *entitlement* is known.

**Possession.** For each km of the 42.9 km Masindi–Biiso lot, the due date of access under three readings:

- (i) the first 30% at day 91, the rest in sequential 5 km sections released evenly to day 546;
- (ii) everything due by day 546;
- (iii) the Addendum 1 milestone for the first section.

The spread of due dates is compared with the satellite clearance date.

**Notices.** Intervals between successive possession notices.

## 4. Results

**Weather: the clock starts before the facts exist.**

- V was positive in 23 of 36 contract months (Fig. 1a). In 17 of these, the rain-day count crossed its normal during the month.
- A 28-day clock from that first excess day expires before the month's gridded record is published in 52% of those months. A contractor relying on public data would be out of time before the data exist. One relying on its own gauge can compute V only at month end.
- Whether a positive month yields any entitlement depends on later months. The running sum peaked at 48.0 days in June 2021 and ended at 38.4 in April 2022: 28.4 of 66.8 positive days were cancelled by later drier months.
- The "event" giving rise to the claim, a net excess over the contract, is therefore knowable only at the end of the contract, long after any 28-day window from its constituent months.
- The Contractor submitted the September 2019–November 2020 weather claim on 4 January 2021. The Engineer recited the 28-day rule and granted 21 days "in line with the Contractor's analysis", without determining when awareness arose.

**Possession: three schedules in one sentence.**

- Day 91 and day 546 fall on 25 July 2019 and 22 October 2020 (the contract record uses 25 October 2020).
- Under reading (i) the due dates step from July 2019 to October 2020; under (ii) every kilometre is due on day 546; under (iii) the first section moves to March 2021 (Fig. 1b).
- The due date of a given km, and so the awareness date of its late possession, differs across readings by a median of 304 days and up to 600.
- The satellite record shows the corridor cleared between January 2021 and April 2022. Under any reading, then, the "event" of late possession began months before the first clearance, but the reading decides by how much.

**Notices: rolling and defensive.**

- For Masindi–Biiso, the EOT 7 file lists notices to claim for pending land on 18 November 2022, 6 January, 25 January and 20 February 2023. The last is dated 2022 in the report, but the letter sequence implies 2023.
- Intervals are 23, 49, 19 and 26 days.
- The Contractor treats late possession as a continuing event and re-notifies roughly within each 28-day window, two years after access was due.
- The rhythm is that of a party protecting itself against an unknowable start date, not one giving early warning.

## 5. Discussion

**The contract creates the indeterminacy.**

- The weather formula's algebraic sum postpones entitlement to the end of the contract, but the notice clock runs from each event.
- The access schedule's "approximately 5 km sections over a period" gives no date to any section.
- In both cases the data are clear. What the contract leaves open is which datum starts the clock.

**Time bars then fail in both directions.** Strictly applied, they would bar weather claims the formula makes unknowable within 28 days. In practice they are not applied: EOT 3 was granted on a single submission covering 15 months, and parties fall back on blanket and rolling notices. Either way the bar no longer serves its purpose, early warning while the facts can still be checked.

**A notice clock.** For data-observable events, Particular Conditions can define awareness by reference to records that exist on known dates:

| Event | Awareness date ("should have become aware") | Data |
|---|---|---|
| Exceptionally adverse climate (formula) | Publication of the month's record (e.g. 20th of following month); one cumulative notice per month, entitlement settled at completion | Named gauge or gridded product |
| Late access / possession | The scheduled date in a section-by-section access table (no "approximately") | Contract Data access schedule; monthly clearance record (Paper 2) |
| Price index / cost change | Release date of the relevant index | UBOS/CIPI release calendar (Paper 1) |
| Government restriction (COVID-type) | Gazettement or publication date of the measure | Official gazette; OxCGRT (Paper 7) |

With these, the Engineer's first check becomes a date comparison. Continuing events would carry one notice and a monthly update, not rolling re-notices.

## 6. Limitations

- One project; notice letters are known only as listed in the claims (contents not reviewed); EOT 3's prior notices are referred to but not dated in the assessment.
- The CHIRPS publication date is approximated. Site-gauge availability would advance awareness to month end, but does not change the algebraic-sum result.
- The possession readings are illustrative. Others (e.g. the Contractor's programme) are possible and would widen the spread.
