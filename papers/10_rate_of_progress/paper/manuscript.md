# How Slow Is "Too Slow"? Progress Benchmarks, Milestones and Recovery Feasibility under FIDIC Sub-Clauses 8.7 and 15.2

Gavamukulya Charles

*Draft 0.1, 15 October 2026. Paper 10 of "Determinable at tender". Target: Journal of Construction Engineering and Management (ASCE); Journal of Legal Affairs and Dispute Resolution in Engineering and Construction (ASCE).*

## Abstract

**Introduction.** FIDIC Sub-Clause 8.7 (1999: 8.6) lets the Engineer require the Contractor to accelerate if progress is "too slow to complete within the Time for Completion". Sub-Clause 15.2 allows termination if the Contractor "fails to proceed with the Works in accordance with Clause 8". Neither defines too slow. Ugandan contracts add a milestone, typically 40% physical progress within 60% of the contract period, with milestone damages.

**Methods.**

- Two Ugandan road contracts are analysed:
  - Project B: 145 km rehabilitation, 2023–, with monthly planned and actual progress;
  - Project A: 97 km design and build, 2019–, with the Engineer's progress snapshots.
- Each is assessed against three benchmarks: time-linear progress, the programme (original or revised), and the 40%-at-60% milestone.
- A recovery-feasibility test asks whether the average monthly progress needed to finish on time exceeds (a) the programme's own peak monthly rate and (b) the Contractor's best demonstrated three-month rate.

**Results.**

- **Benchmarks give different verdicts.** In February 2021, at 59% of time, Project A was 38 points behind time-linear progress but only 1.7 points behind its revised programme.
- **The milestone is lenient.** Project B's programme planned 63% at 60% of time, so the milestone tolerates progress 23 points behind plan.
- **Project B became unrecoverable early.** Finishing on time required more than the programme's peak rate (5.5% a month) from January 2025. It required more than the Contractor's best demonstrated rate from October 2023, five months after commencement.
- **Project B, July 2025** (75% of time, 21.2% complete): 8.8% a month was needed (4.4% with the recommended 273-day extension), against a best demonstrated 2.3%. The Contractor had reduced the rate of work under Sub-Clause 16.1 because UGX 26 bn of certificates were unpaid.
- **Drafting defect.** The Auditor General found the milestone clause on Kampala city roads defective: damages were forfeited where the 40% milestone was met but completion was late.

**Discussion.** "Too slow" is determinable once the contract names the benchmark and the recoverability test. Proposed clause:

- progress measured against the accepted programme as updated for granted extensions;
- a Notice to Correct triggered when the required rate exceeds the programme's peak rate for two consecutive months;
- milestones set on the programme's own curve, with milestone damages that survive completion;
- a statement that a rate reduction under 16.1 suspends the test for its duration.

## 1. Introduction

Termination for slow progress is the gravest remedy in a construction contract and among the most litigated. FIDIC's tests are qualitative:

- 8.6/8.7: progress "too slow to complete within the Time for Completion";
- 8.1: "proceed with the Works with due expedition and without delay";
- 15.2: "fails to proceed in accordance with Clause 8".

Each requires a counterfactual: could the remaining work still be finished on time? Each also depends on a benchmark: the original programme, the latest revised programme, or simple proportion of time.

Ugandan particular conditions add an objective milestone with its own damages: completion of 30% of the road length (Project B) or 40% of the works (Project E) within 60% of the original period. This paper asks:

1. How far do verdicts depend on the benchmark?
2. Where does the milestone sit relative to the programmes it is meant to police?
3. Can recovery feasibility be measured early enough to act on?

## 2. Data

- **Project B** (anonymised; FIDIC 2017-based, MoWT/UNRA):
  - commenced 10 May 2023; Time for Completion 36 months (9 May 2026); contract price UGX 309 bn;
  - monthly planned and actual physical progress, May 2023–July 2025 (Monthly Progress Report 23);
  - status at 31 August 2025: 21.55% actual vs 90.56% planned at 77% of time;
  - the Engineer recommended a 273-day EOT for increased scope;
  - the Contractor reduced its rate of work under Sub-Clause 16.1 after IPCs of UGX 26 bn went unpaid.
- **Project A** (FIDIC 1999 Yellow Book): Engineer's progress snapshots in the EOT assessments. February 2021: 58.73% of time, 22.29% planned (revised programme), 20.56% actual. December 2021: 81.50%, 61.10%, 54.05%.
- **Auditor General** (FY2024/25): engineering audit of Kampala city roads contracts with the 40%-in-60% milestone.

## 3. Methods

- **Benchmarks.** Progress gap at time t: (i) time-linear (t − p); (ii) programme (plan_t − p); (iii) milestone (p ≥ 40% at t = 60%).
- **Recovery feasibility.** Required average rate r_req(t) = (100 − p_t) / (T − t), for T = original Time for Completion and T + 273 days. This is compared with:
  - (a) the programme's peak monthly rate (5.5%);
  - (b) the Contractor's best three-month average monthly rate achieved to date, from month 6.

  The first month in which r_req exceeds each is recorded.

## 4. Results

**Benchmarks decide the verdict.** In February 2021 Project A had executed 20.6% at 58.7% of time:

- 38.2 points behind time-linear progress;
- 1.7 points behind the revised programme.

By December 2021 the figures were 27.4 and 7.1 points. Measured against its own revised programme, the Contractor was almost on schedule. Measured against time, it had a 38-point gap. The revised programme absorbed the delay.

**The milestone sits far below the plan.**

- Project B's programme planned 62.9% at 60% of time. The 40% milestone therefore allows a 23-point shortfall against plan before any consequence.
- Project B still missed it, with 16.4% at 60% of time.
- Project A, with a back-loaded revised programme, would have met a 40% milestone by December 2021 (54% at 81.5% of time). It was 22 points behind time-linear progress at that point.

**Recovery became infeasible early on Project B.** On the Contractor's own demonstrated rate, completion on time was infeasible from October 2023, the sixth month, even allowing for the 273-day extension. Required progress exceeded the programme's peak monthly rate from January 2025, 20 months in (Fig. 1b). By July 2025 completion required 8.8% a month to the original date, or 4.4% with the extension, against a best demonstrated 2.3%.

**Employer default interacts.** From mid-2025 the Contractor reduced its rate of work under Sub-Clause 16.1 for unpaid certificates (UGX 26 bn; see Paper 9). Slow progress caused by the Employer's payment default is not progress "without reasonable excuse". Any 8.7 or 15.2 test must therefore net out periods of 16.1 reduction, which the contract does not do.

**The milestone clause can defeat delay damages.** The Auditor General found, on Kampala city roads, that the delay-damages clause "could not be applied if the contractor achieved 40% physical progress in 60% of the allocated time but failed to complete the works within the overall completion timeline resulting in forfeiting of the damages". A milestone meant to add a check on progress instead removed the main remedy for late completion.

## 5. Discussion

**Benchmark choice is the dispute.** Progress measured against a programme revised after the event shows little delay. Measured against time it shows a lot. Measured against a 40% milestone it often shows none. The contract names none of these as *the* test for 8.7/15.2, so the Engineer and the parties argue about the benchmark rather than the progress.

**Recovery feasibility is measurable and early.** The required rate and the demonstrated rate are both in every monthly report. On Project B they showed, in the first year, that completion on time was not credible at the rates achieved. That was 18 months before the milestone date and two years before any termination question would arise.

**A determinable clause.**

1. **Benchmark.** Progress is measured against the accepted programme, updated only for extensions of time granted under 8.5. Contractor-driven re-sequencing does not reset the benchmark.
2. **Trigger.** If the required average rate to complete exceeds the programme's peak monthly rate for two consecutive monthly reports, the Engineer issues an 8.7 instruction. If it then exceeds 1.5 times the Contractor's best demonstrated three-month rate, the Employer may issue a 15.1 Notice to Correct.
3. **Milestones on the curve.** Milestone percentages are set from the accepted programme (e.g. plan − 10 points at 60%), not a fixed 40%. Milestone damages are deducted provisionally and are not forfeited by later progress, only credited against delay damages at completion.
4. **Employer default.** Periods of reduced rate under 16.1 are excluded from the tests.

## 6. Limitations

- Two projects; Project A data are snapshots.
- "Physical progress" is a value-weighted measure that may diverge from critical-path progress.
- Satellite verification of reported progress (Paper 2's Sentinel-2 pipeline applied to surfacing) is planned.
- The recovery test ignores learning and remobilisation effects and is an indicator, not a schedule analysis.
